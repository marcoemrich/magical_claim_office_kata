# The Most Honorable Privileged Claims Office for Magical Risks and Cursed Items

— or, since even assessors struggle with that: the MHPCO.

> *Deutsche Fassung: [`kata.md`](kata.md)*

## A brief introduction

The MHPCO is one of the oldest insurance institutions in the twelve
kingdoms and holds a privilege that nobody has seen in the original for
a very long time — which does not stop it from quoting the thing on
every letterhead.

Its business consists, in essence, of insuring magical items. These
include swords, amulets, staves, potions and all manner of components —
runes, moonstones and occasionally more exotic things the house rules
would rather not discuss in detail. The assessor who performs the
initial inspection has a small room on the ground floor for this
purpose, and a very good supply of candles.

The MHPCO is, depending on where you stand, *conscientious* or
*stingy* — both are true, according to whether you are on the counter
side or the customer side. What the house rules describe as "prudent
management in the interest of the insured", common speech describes in
less friendly words. Neither view is entirely unfounded.

## What the claims office insures

The MHPCO distinguishes between *main items* and *components*. The main
items — swords and their closer relatives — appear in the official
price list:

- a sword is carried at an insured value of 1000 G, with a base premium
  of 100 G;
- an amulet at 600 G and 60 G;
- a staff at 800 G and 80 G;
- a potion at 400 G and 40 G.

On request the assessor will also examine unusual pieces — the
*Seven-Armed Staff of Dawn, Witness of the Third Turning of Ages*, for
instance — but in ninety-two cases out of a hundred concludes that the
object is a *staff* and that the official price applies. The ledger
reads "staff, wood, 800 G".

Components — that is, runes, moonstones and their like — are insured at
250 G each, with a base premium of 25 G apiece. There is a small tariff
advantage for those customers who deliver their components in proper
sets: a *building block* of three alike components costs a flat 60 G in
base premium. That is fifteen gold pieces below the individual price
and is widely held in the office to be the most generous concession the
MHPCO has ever granted.

## Calculating the premium

The calculation begins plainly with the sum of the base premiums of all
insured pieces. To this are then applied a series of surcharges and
discounts that the office has levied for generations.

**Cursed items** attract a risk surcharge of 50 %. Over the centuries
the MHPCO has learned that cursed swords fall upon legs, door frames
and occasionally the furniture of the claims office with disagreeable
regularity. The surcharge is a kind of advance compensation for the
efforts such pieces inevitably cause.

**Highly enchanted items** — those with an enchantment level of five or
more — carry a further surcharge of 30 %. The precise definition of
"enchantment level" has changed several times over the decades. The
current rule: whatever the mage writes on the label counts. Anyone
registering a sword at level *one thousand* will be politely asked by
the assessor to confine themselves to the official scale.

**Long-standing customers** — those whose business relationship with
the MHPCO has lasted at least two years — receive a loyalty discount of
20 %. The MHPCO calls this a *loyalty discount*; it would probably be
more honest to describe it as a token of appreciation for having
endured two full years of dealings at the counter.

**A first insurance** attracts an *initial assessment surcharge* of
10 %. This covers the effort of the assessor, who must inspect the
piece, smell it, and enter it in the ledger.

**Follow-up contracts** — that is, every contract of a customer after
their first — receive a discount of 15 % on the premium. This is how
the office keeps its regulars on the hook without unsettling them with
actual generosity.

Finally, a **processing fee** of 5 G is added to every premium. Common
speech calls it *the stamp fee*, which is not entirely inaccurate;
the office does in fact possess two very old stamps, the operation of
which occupies the staff in question for several seconds.

> *Note by the actuary (handwritten, unofficial):*
> "All amounts are rounded to whole gold pieces in the office's favour.
> That is not a rule, that is a custom."

## In the event of damage

Should it come to pass that an insured item comes to harm — through
dragon fire, through one's own clumsiness, through what the office
discreetly calls a *concatenation of unfortunate circumstances* — the
policyholder files a claim.

In every such case the MHPCO retains a **deductible** of 100 G. The
house rules explain this by saying that the deductible "shall encourage
the insured to treat the items entrusted to him with due care". Anyone
who asks the actuary in person receives the somewhat less elegant
answer that the MHPCO simply likes to retain a hundred gold pieces
wherever it can.

For particularly conspicuous pieces the office knows two special
clauses:

- **Strongly enchanted items** — those with an enchantment level of
  eight or higher — are reimbursed at only 50 % of the damage amount.
  Over the years the assessor has repeatedly observed that such pieces
  behave in a manner that is, so to speak, *self-healing* as soon as the
  insurance is out of sight; the clause takes account of this.

- **Items made of dragon material** are reimbursed in full. The MHPCO
  is fond of stressing that this is a particularly *generous* rule;
  what the MHPCO does not mention is that dragon material is generally
  so robust that the clause rarely comes into play at all.

## The task

You work — congratulations! — on the administrative system of the
MHPCO. Your assignment is modest:

- Calculate the *premium* for a list of items (in the office this is
  simply called *quote*).
- *Settle a claim* against an existing policy and determine the payout
  (the *claim*).
